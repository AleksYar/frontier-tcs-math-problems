                                                               MINIMAL DISTANCE PROBLEM

                                                   Abstract. We show that for every n ∈ N there is a collection of points p1 , . . . , pn and lines ℓ1 , . . . , ℓn
                                                   in the unit square such that for any i we have pi ∈ ℓi and the distance from pi to any other line ℓj

                                                                                               1. Introduction
                                               In [2], Cohen, Pohoata and the second author considered the following problem about collections
                                           of point-line pairs. For an integer n ⩾ 2, what is the largest δ > 0 such that there are points
                                           p1 , . . . , pn ∈ [0, 1]2 and lines ℓ1 , . . . , ℓn ⊂ R2 such that pi ∈ ℓi and for every i ̸= j we have d(pi , ℓj ) ⩾ δ?
                                           By taking pi = (i/n, 0) and ℓi = pi + (0, 1)R it is easy too see that δ ⩾ 1/n. On the other hand, by

                                           δ ⩽ [√n−1] .
                                              In [2], authors managed to improve this simple upper bound to δ ⩽ n−2/3+o(1) . As a corollary, they
                                           obtained a new upper bound for Heilbronn’s triangle problem which asks to determine the smallest
                                           number ∆(n) such that among any n points in unit square there exists a triangle with area at most
                                           ∆(n). The connection between these two problems can be seen as follows: given a set of n points
                                           in [0, 1]2 we can find a collection of at least n/10 disjoint pairs {pi , qi } among these points so that
                                           d(pi , qi ) ⩽ 2n−1/2 for all i. Let ℓi be the line passing through pi , qi and note that if d(pi , ℓj ) ⩽ δ for
                                           some i ̸= j then the points pi , pj , qj form a triangle of area at most ∆ ⩽ 12 d(pi , ℓj )d(pj , qj ) ⩽ n−1/2 δ.
                                           So better upper bounds on the minimal distance δ lead to better upper bounds on the area of smallest
                                           triangle ∆. In particular, the upper bound δ ⩽ n−2/3+o(1) gives ∆ ⩽ n−7/6+o(1) . This estimate
                                           improves on previous work on Heilbronn’s triangle problem by Roth [5], [6], Komlós–Pintz–Szemerédi
                                           [3] and Cohen, Pohoata and the second author [1] and reaches a certain ‘high-low’ limit of the method.
                                              Many authors (see e.g. the table on page 34 in [1]) have guessed that the bound ∆(n) ≲ n−3/2 should
                                           hold based on the following heuristic. Let ℓj = pj qj be lines defined as above and let Hj ⊂ [0, 1]2 be
                                           the strip of points x ∈ [0, 1]2 at distance at most ⩽ K/n to ℓj . If the points p1 , . . . , pn were uniformly
                                           distributed in [0, 1]2 then we would expect that about |Hj |n ∼ K of them would land in the strip
                                           Hj . So for K ≫ 1 there should be lots of points in the strip Hj and so we should be able to find a
                                           triangle with area at most 2n−1/2 (K/n) ≲ n−3/2 . One way to turn this idea into a rigorous proof is to
                                           show that in the setup in the first paragraph, we have the upper bound δ ≲ n−1 . Because of this, it
                                           seemed plausible to the authors of [2] that the upper bound δ ⩽ n−1+o(1) holds. In Appendix B of [2],
                                           they further note that this estimate, if true, would also imply non-trivial upper bounds for Heilbronn’s
                                           problem for k-gons.
                                              In this note, we show that this is not the case, namely that there are collections of point-line pairs
                                           with δ ≳ nγ−1 for some (small) constant γ > 0.
                                           Theorem 1.1. There exist constants c, γ > 0 such that for any n ⩾ 2 there are points p1 , . . . , pn ∈
                                           [0, 1]2 and lines ℓ1 , . . . , ℓn such that pi ∈ ℓi and d(pi , ℓj ) ⩾ cnγ−1 for any i ̸= j.

triangle problem. However it does hint at the possibility that there could be better lower bound
constructions: the current best lower bound ∆(n) ≳ log        n2

   In the next section we present the proof of Theorem 1.1. The proof consists of two parts: first, we use
the probabilistic method to beat the lower bound by a logarithmic factor and second, we use a recursive
construction to amplify this improvement into a polynomial gain. In particular, the set of points and
lines we construct is essentially a self-affine fractal. Self-affine fractals might be an interesting special
class of configurations for further study of the minimal distance problem.

                                                      2. Proof
   To ease the recursive construction it is convenient to introduce the following notation (very similar
to the one used in [2]). Let Ω = [−1, 1]2 × [−1, 1], where we view Ω as the configuration space of
incident point-line pairs in the plane. Namely, for (p, θ) ∈ Ω let ℓ = ℓ(p, θ) = p + (1, θ)R be the
line with slope θ through p. So we can identify a subset X ⊂ Ω with the point-line configuration
{(p, ℓ(p, θ)), (p, θ) ∈ X}.
   For ω = (x, y, θ), ω ′ = (x′ , y ′ , θ′ ) ∈ Ω define the vertical distance d(ω, ω ′ ) = |y − y ′ − θ′ (x − x′ )|. For
ω, ω ′ ∈ Ω, d(ω, ω ′ ) measures the vertical distance from the point p = (x, y) to the line ℓ′ = ℓ′ (p′ , θ′ ),
with p′ = (x′ , y ′ ). Since |θ′ | ⩽ 1 it coincides with the euclidean distance up to a constant factor. For a
point-line configuration X ⊂ Ω define the minimal distance of X as
